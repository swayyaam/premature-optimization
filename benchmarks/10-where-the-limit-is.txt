Step 10: looking for the bottleneck before scaling the database

Date:    2026-09-04 18:37 IST
Machine: Apple M5, 10 cores, macOS 26.6.2

Every measurement in this repo until now used fifty concurrent requests. These
three sweeps ask what happens at other levels, and where the limit actually is.
MAX_IN_FLIGHT was raised to 8192 so the in flight limit from step 8 could not be
what was being measured.

  hey -z 10s -c <concurrency> -disable-redirects <url>

=================== 1. redirects, cache on, by concurrency ===================

  conc   req/s       mean_ms    p99_ms
  25     42098       0.60       0.90
  50     42824       1.20       1.60
  100    42507       2.40       2.90
  200    42118       4.70       5.60
  400    42327       9.40       11.30
  800    41321       19.30      22.90

Throughput is the same from 25 concurrent to 800, a range of 32 times, while
mean and 99th percentile latency rise in proportion to the concurrency. That is
what a hard ceiling looks like. Adding waiting clients does not produce more
work done, it only makes each client wait longer for its turn.

(The CPU figures printed by this sweep were sampled after each run rather than
during it, so they are not reliable and have been left out. The during-run
figures are in sweep 3 below and in benchmarks/05-cache-hit.txt.)

============ 2. the same sweep against an endpoint that does nothing ============

  /healthz (no round trip to anything) vs /{code} (one round trip)
  conc   healthz      redirect    
  50     90424        42151       
  200    90131        42681       
  800    98386        42120       

/healthz returns a fixed string and talks to nothing. It is flat at about 90,000
at every concurrency. The redirect endpoint is flat at about 42,000. The only
difference between the two is that a redirect makes one round trip to a backend
over a socket.

=================== 3. redirects, cache off, by concurrency ===================

  conc   req/s       p99_ms     postgres_cpu_during
  50     42039       5.10       227%
  200    42060       20.60      224%
  800    42012       85.90      222%

With the cache off, every one of those requests is a query to Postgres, and the
ceiling is the same 42,000. Postgres held steady at about 225% of CPU on a
machine with ten cores, sampled while the runs were in progress. It was not
saturated at any point. It was doing the same amount of work per second at 800
concurrent as at 50, because that is all the application handed it.

------------------------------------ so ------------------------------------

The ceiling is the same whether the round trip goes to Redis or to Postgres, and
the backend at the far end is idle in the first case and unsaturated in the
second. What costs 48,000 requests per second is the round trip itself, not what
answers it.

A read replica adds somewhere else to send a round trip. It cannot make a round
trip cheaper, and the database was never the thing setting the limit.
